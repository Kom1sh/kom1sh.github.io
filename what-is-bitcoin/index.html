What is a bitcoin? The honest answer.

Strip away the jargon and bitcoin is one thing: an entry on a public ledger that no single party controls. Everything else — the price, the mining, the politics — follows from that design.

A ledger, not a coin

There is no bitcoin "file" and nothing physical to hold. The Bitcoin blockchain is a shared accounting ledger — a list of who can spend what — replicated across tens of thousands of computers (nodes) worldwide. When someone "owns" bitcoin, they control a private key: a secret number that lets them sign transactions moving entries on that ledger. Lose the key, and the coins sit on the ledger forever, unspendable. Roughly 19.9 million BTC have been issued since the network launched on January 3, 2009; a hard-coded cap of 21 million will be approached around the year 2140.

BTC is simply the ticker's shorthand — the way exchanges quote one unit of bitcoin, the same way USD quotes a dollar. Each BTC divides into 100 million satoshis ("sats"), so at a price of $114,650, one US dollar buys about 872 sats. You never need to buy a whole coin.

How a transaction actually works

Say you pay someone 0.01 BTC. Your wallet software builds a transaction, signs it with your private key, and broadcasts it. Within seconds, thousands of nodes check the signature and confirm you control the funds. Miners then bundle your transaction with others into a block — roughly every ten minutes — and race to seal that block with a proof-of-work computation that currently costs enormous amounts of electricity. That cost is the security model: rewriting history would require redoing all that work faster than the rest of the network combined, which has never happened. After a few blocks, the payment is effectively irreversible. No card network, no bank, no business hours.

Who's in charge? Nobody — on purpose

Bitcoin's rules — the 21 million cap, the ten-minute block target, the halving every 210,000 blocks — are enforced by the independent nodes, not declared by a foundation. Changing them requires the people running those nodes to voluntarily adopt new software. Contentious changes have been attempted and have failed; the supply cap has never moved in seventeen years. This is why bitcoin is described as "credibly neutral": its monetary policy is closer to a physical constant than to a corporate decision.

Why it has value at all

Bitcoin pays no yield and backs no cash flows, so its value rests on scarcity plus demand — like gold, but with a supply schedule anyone can audit in seconds. As of October 2026 the network's market value is about $2.28T, held by individuals, ETFs, corporations and a handful of governments. Skeptics note, correctly, that the price has fallen 70%+ four separate times. Believers note, also correctly, that every prior cycle low has eventually been exceeded. Both facts belong in any honest answer to "should I buy bitcoin?"

Frequently asked questions

What does BTC mean?

BTC is the ticker symbol for one bitcoin, used on exchanges worldwide. A smaller unit, the satoshi, is one hundred-millionth of a BTC.

Is bitcoin real money?

It functions as money in specific contexts — it is legal tender in El Salvador and settles billions of dollars of transfers daily — but most holders treat it as a scarce asset rather than spending money.

Can Bitcoin be shut down?

No single government can switch it off, because the ledger exists on thousands of machines in over a hundred countries. States can restrict on-ramps like exchanges, and some have, but the network itself has run uninterrupted since 2009.
