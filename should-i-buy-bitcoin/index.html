Should I buy bitcoin now? The honest version.

The truthful answer is that nobody knows — not us, not the ETF salesmen, not the permabears. What we can give you is the actual trade-off, with numbers, and a framework for deciding.

Satoshi Pulse is a news and education desk. Nothing on this page is investment advice, a recommendation, or a solicitation. Bitcoin is a volatile asset that can lose most of its value. Never invest money you cannot afford to lose.

The case for buying

Bitcoin's supply is the one number in finance that nobody can print: 21 million coins, with 19.93 million already issued and daily new supply of roughly 450 BTC after the April 2024 halving. Against that fixed supply, demand has structurally broadened — US spot ETFs hold over 1.3 million BTC, more than 140 public companies carry bitcoin on their balance sheets, and the market cap of about $2.28T is still under a tenth of gold's. Every four-year cycle so far has eventually exceeded the last one's peak: $1,100 in 2013, ~$19,700 in 2017, ~$69,000 in 2021, ~$126,000 in 2025. Buyers of every prior all-time high who simply held were made whole within three years.

The case against buying

The same price history that shows recoveries shows the cost of waiting for them: drawdowns of 78–94% in four separate cycles, with multi-year stretches underwater. Bitcoin has no cash flows to anchor a valuation, so in a liquidity squeeze it falls harder than almost any mainstream asset — 2022 took it from $69,000 to $15,500 while inflation ran hot, embarrassing the "inflation hedge" pitch. Regulation remains a live risk in every jurisdiction. And the current cycle is untested territory: shallower drawdowns in 2024–2026 may reflect a maturing market, or may simply mean the real purge hasn't happened yet. Anyone selling you certainty in either direction is selling.

The volatility math nobody shows you

A 50% drawdown requires a 100% gain to recover. Bitcoin has delivered both, repeatedly — but only for holders who didn't sell at the bottom. The practical implication: your position size should be an amount you can watch halve without flinching, because historically that is not a tail risk, it is the base case. Institutional allocators typically frame bitcoin exposure at 1–5% of a portfolio for exactly this reason — large enough to matter, small enough to survive.

Dollar-cost averaging, explained properly

DCA means buying a fixed dollar amount on a fixed schedule — say $100 every Monday — regardless of price. When bitcoin is expensive, your $100 buys fewer sats; when it crashes, it buys more. Your average entry converges toward the average price over the period, which removes the single biggest risk for retail buyers: deploying a lump sum the week before a 40% correction. DCA doesn't beat perfect timing — nothing does — but it has historically beaten almost every attempt at actual timing, and it converts an emotional decision into a mechanical one. Questions to answer before you buy anything

  • Can this money stay invested for four years — one full cycle — without being needed?
  • Would a 50% loss change my life, or just my mood? Only the second is acceptable.
  • Do I understand what I own well enough to hold through a crash? The explainer is a starting point.
  • Am I buying because of a thesis, or because the price went up this week?

If the answers check out, the mechanics are simple: a regulated exchange or a spot ETF in a brokerage account, a recurring buy, and the discipline to ignore the daily quote in between.
